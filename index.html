What Can One Sentence Fix?

A Failure Taxonomy and a Minimal Language Correction for Frozen VLA Policies

Anonymous Author(s)

Under review

Paper (Coming Soon) Code (Coming Soon)

One sentence per task, written once after a failed rollout and replayed on every later rollout — the frozen policy does the rest.

Abstract

When a deployed vision-language-action (VLA) policy fails, the person watching it can usually say what went wrong in one sentence. We ask what such a sentence can and cannot fix. We label every failing LIBERO-90 task (out of distribution) for two policies, OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5, and find that the failures sort into four modes. One of them, execution misalignment, is the one a single sentence can reach, where the policy reaches the right target but miscalibrates how far or how hard to move, and the other three lack a target or a plan, not a magnitude.

For the correctable mode we define the narrowest human correction that can reach it: one retrospective sentence per task, grounded by an LLM into an action dimension, direction, magnitude term, and time window, and added as a bias to the frozen policy’s actions. Re-executed on every failed rollout, it recovers 65 of 139 in-distribution and 33 of 328 out-of-distribution failures in simulation. On a real xArm7 after the robot base is moved, one sentence per task raises pick-and-place from 10/30 to 30/30 in each of two same-object conditions and from 2/30 to 24/30 with a new object, while two long-horizon tasks stay at 0/30 with and without correction, as the taxonomy predicts.

A naively prompted VLM given the same failure video fails to recover a single case: the value of the correction is the person’s calibrated sense of magnitude and timing, not language itself. A correction touches less than a second of a trajectory, not the whole of it: because a policy’s miscalibration recurs across episodes, fixing that one moment once lets the policy complete the rest with its own reasoning, episode after episode.

Four Findings

1. A failure taxonomy that is stable across architectures

On 133 failing instructions, OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5 fail in the same four ways in nearly the same proportions, and only one mode, execution misalignment, keeps the task structure intact.

2. The task, not the episode, as the unit of correction

One retrospective sentence per task, added as a bias, is reused on every failed rollout and every real-robot run.

3. The boundary holds on hardware from both sides

After an environment shift, one sentence lifts pick-and-place to 10/10 with the same object, but advanced tasks stay at 0/30.

4. The information is in the person

The correction written by GPT-5 mini from the same video does not recover any trial; what the person supplies is calibrated timing.

Failure Taxonomy and Correctability

We ran OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5, both fine-tuned on the same 40 LIBERO tasks (Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10), on all 90 LIBERO-90 tasks, none of which is in that set. LIBERO-90 is therefore out of distribution in its instructions, not its scenes. Every instruction with at least one failed rollout was labelled by one annotator with the key failure frame and one of four modes: 73 of the 74 instructions for OpenVLA-OFT and 60 for π0.5 (holding all 328 of its 450 failed rollouts), 133 instruction-model pairs in total.

The operational boundary. The distribution is nearly identical across the two architectures, and only execution misalignment keeps the right target and plan, which is what a magnitude correction assumes. A magnitude bias reaches execution misalignment and nothing else, because the other modes lack a target or a plan, not a magnitude. Read the other way, it says what a robot should ask a person for: a magnitude in one mode, a target or a plan in the others.

Human Correction: A Minimal Signal for the Correctable Mode

A frozen policy πθ outputs 7-D actions (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw, gripper). A rollout fails; a person watches it once and writes K sentences, K small. We seek a bias δ such that re-executing with at + δt succeeds — with the weights frozen, no new data, and the same δ reused for every later rollout of the task. “Move left more in 1 to 2 seconds” becomes (y, −1, more, [1.0, 2.0]). That is the whole of what the person supplies: explicit, retrospective, expressed in the policy’s action space rather than as a goal or preference, sparse (one or two sentences for most tasks, never more than five), and located in time.

One sentence per task. Failure (red), the correction, success (green). Top: “down little, 2.0–2.3 s” recovers a missed bowl grasp. Bottom: “left slightly” and “down slightly, 0.0–0.5 s” recover a drawer that was never opened.

Grounding

Each sentence is grounded by GPT-5 mini into a 4-tuple: action dimension (x/y/z/roll/pitch/yaw/grip), sign (±1), magnitude term, and time window [tstart, tend].

Magnitude

The term maps to a signed weight from fixed ranges — slightly [0.3, 0.5], more [0.5, 0.8], much [0.8, 1.0] — set on held-out corrections before evaluation. The person never types a number.

Timing

A piecewise-linear envelope rises from 0 at tstart to 1 at the window midpoint and back to 0 at tend. Corrections on the same dimension superpose and are added to the policy’s action.

Action-space adaptation. Corrections are specified in Cartesian space to match how people speak (“move left,” “move up”). The real xArm7 runs under joint-velocity control, so a Cartesian correction is mapped to joint velocities through the Jacobian pseudoinverse — the minimum-norm joint velocity that realizes the requested end-effector motion.

What the signal assumes. (A1) The policy has the right target and plan; only how far or how hard is wrong. (A2) A few biases turn the near-miss into a success. (A3) One viewing shows a person which dimension drifted and when. The taxonomy measures A1; the experiments below test A2 and A3.

Does the Signal Suffice? Simulation

Simulation uses LIBERO with the lerobot/pi05_libero_finetuned checkpoint of π0.5. We compare the base policy; a VLM baseline (GPT-5 mini, naive prompting) that watches the failure video and writes the same 4-tuple with no human input; and CorrectVLA, where a person writes one task-level correction reused across all rollouts. Corrections are applied only to execution-misalignment tasks. The base policy runs first and the correction is re-executed on its failed episodes only, so success counts add recovered failures to base successes. We run 50 trials on each of the 40 LIBERO tasks in distribution and 5 trials on each of the 90 LIBERO-90 tasks out of distribution.

Does the Signal Suffice? Real Robot

Real-robot experiments use a UFactory xArm7 with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and RealSense cameras. π0.5-DROID was fine-tuned in one workspace, where it reached 95.0% on pick-and-place across 20 trials. We then moved the robot base, shifting camera viewpoint and workspace geometry, and ran each condition three times with 10 trials each.

Real robot. After the shift π0.5 misses both grasps (red); one task-level correction restores success (green).

What Does the Human Supply?

Can feedback from a foundation model replace feedback from a person? For this channel, not yet. The VLM baseline isolates the question: it sees the same failure video, writes the same 4-tuple, and recovers nothing. The value lies in two judgments the person makes from one viewing and the VLM did not: how large the miss was in the robot’s action units, and when it happened.

Most corrected tasks succeeded with the first correction; second rounds only adjusted the magnitude term. The person’s intervention was mainly timing: a window of a few tenths of a second around the missed contact.

Discussion: A Channel That Is Not Retained

The channel is misspecified for the failures that dominate

Over 70% of failing out-of-distribution tasks lack a target or a plan, and an additive bias cannot say “the other bowl” nor fold a shirt. The channel cannot represent what the person means, so no feedback converges. These are the modes where foundation models generalize well.

Nothing is learned

The correction is written by a person and replayed as given; every new shift or task needs a new sentence. The open problem is intent propagation: can a policy retain what a correction expresses and generalize it beyond the situation in which it was said?

Limitations and Conclusion

Limitations. The taxonomy was labelled by one annotator; a task-level correction assumes one failure pattern per task; the corrections were written by the authors; there is no user study; corrections are language only, with no multimodal feedback or depth sensing; and the bias was not applied to episodes the base policy already completed.

Conclusion. A generalist VLA under distribution shift fails in four ways, and only execution misalignment leaves the target and the plan intact. For that mode the smallest correction a person can give, one retrospective sentence per task, is enough: added as a bias to the frozen policy, it recovers failures in simulation and on a real robot after an environment shift, and a foundation model given the same video does not supply what the person did. Two lessons follow: diagnose the mode before choosing the channel, and treat the sentence as information a policy should keep — today it is replayed, never learned.
