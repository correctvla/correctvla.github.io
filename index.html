Abstract

When a deployed vision-language-action (VLA) policy fails, the person watching it can usually say what went wrong in one sentence. We ask what such a sentence can and cannot fix. We label every failing LIBERO-90 task (out of distribution) for two policies, OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5, and find that the failures sort into four modes. One of them, execution misalignment, is the one a single sentence can reach, where the policy reaches the right target but miscalibrates how far or how hard to move, and the other three lack a target or a plan, not a magnitude.

For the correctable mode we define the narrowest human correction that can reach it: one retrospective sentence per task, grounded by an LLM into an action dimension, direction, magnitude term, and time window, and added as a bias to the frozen policy’s actions. Re-executed on every failed rollout, it recovers 65 of 139 in-distribution and 33 of 328 out-of-distribution failures in simulation. On a real xArm7 after the robot base is moved, one sentence per task raises pick-and-place from 10/30 to 30/30 in each of two same-object conditions and from 2/30 to 24/30 with a new object, while two long-horizon tasks stay at 0/30 with and without correction, as the taxonomy predicts.

A naively prompted VLM given the same failure video fails to recover a single case: the value of the correction is the person’s calibrated sense of magnitude and timing, not language itself. A correction touches less than a second of a trajectory, not the whole of it: because a policy’s miscalibration recurs across episodes, fixing that one moment once lets the policy complete the rest with its own reasoning, episode after episode.

Human correction

One sentence becomes a short bias on a frozen policy.

A frozen policy outputs 7-D actions (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw, gripper). A rollout fails; a person watches it once and writes a few sentences. We seek a bias that turns the near-miss into a success when added to the policy’s actions — weights frozen, no new data, and the same bias reused for every later rollout of the task. The person never types a number.

Action-space adaptation

Corrections are specified in Cartesian space to match how people speak (“move left,” “move up”). The real xArm7 runs under joint-velocity control, so a Cartesian correction is mapped to joint velocities through the Jacobian pseudoinverse — the minimum-norm joint velocity that realizes the requested end-effector motion.

What the signal assumes

  • A1 The policy has the right target and plan; only how far or how hard is wrong. Measured by the taxonomy
  • A2 A few biases turn the near-miss into a success. Tested in results
  • A3 One viewing shows a person which dimension drifted and when. Tested against a VLM

Does the signal suffice?

Where the sentence reaches, it usually suffices.

Simulation — LIBERO, π0.5

We compare the base policy (lerobot/pi05_libero_finetuned); a VLM baseline (GPT-5 mini, naive prompting) that watches the failure video and writes the same 4-tuple with no human input; and CorrectVLA, where a person writes one task-level correction reused across all rollouts. Corrections are applied only to execution-misalignment tasks and re-executed on the base policy’s failed episodes: 50 trials on each of 40 LIBERO tasks in distribution, 5 on each of 90 LIBERO-90 tasks out of distribution.

Real robot — xArm7, π0.5-DROID

A UFactory xArm7 with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and RealSense cameras. π0.5-DROID was fine-tuned in one workspace, where it reached 95.0% on pick-and-place across 20 trials. We then moved the robot base, shifting camera viewpoint and workspace geometry, and ran each condition three times with 10 trials each.

The other side of the boundary. Two long-horizon tasks, folding a shirt (92.5% before the shift) and stacking boxes, fail in all 30 trials after the shift, with and without correction: outside execution misalignment, the sentence has nothing to reach.

For this channel, feedback from a foundation model cannot yet replace feedback from a person. The value lies in two judgments the person makes from one viewing and the VLM did not: how large the miss was in the robot’s action units, and when it happened.

Most corrected tasks succeeded with the first correction; second rounds only adjusted the magnitude term. The person’s intervention was mainly timing: a window of a few tenths of a second around the missed contact.

Discussion

A channel that is not retained.

The channel is misspecified for the failures that dominate

Over 70% of failing out-of-distribution tasks lack a target or a plan, and an additive bias cannot say “the other bowl” nor fold a shirt. The channel cannot represent what the person means, so no feedback converges. These are the modes where foundation models generalize well.

Nothing is learned

The correction is written by a person and replayed as given; every new shift or task needs a new sentence. The open problem is intent propagation: can a policy retain what a correction expresses and generalize it beyond the situation in which it was said?

Limitations

  • The taxonomy was labelled by one annotator.
  • A task-level correction assumes one failure pattern per task.
  • The corrections were written by the authors; there is no user study.
  • Corrections are language only, with no multimodal feedback or depth sensing.
  • The bias was not applied to episodes the base policy already completed.

Conclusion

A generalist VLA under distribution shift fails in four ways, and only execution misalignment leaves the target and the plan intact. For that mode the smallest correction a person can give, one retrospective sentence per task, is enough: added as a bias to the frozen policy, it recovers failures in simulation and on a real robot after an environment shift, and a foundation model given the same video does not supply what the person did. Two lessons follow:

1Diagnose the mode before choosing the channel.

2Treat the sentence as information a policy should keep — today it is replayed, never learned.
